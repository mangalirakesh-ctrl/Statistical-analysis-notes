1. Measures of Central Tendency & Dispersion4 Hours

Measures of Central Tendency

Central tendency measures tell us where the "center" or "typical" value of our data lies. Think of it as finding the most representative value in a dataset.

1. Mean (Average)

The mean is the sum of all values divided by the number of values. It's the most commonly used measure.

Mean = (Sum of all values) / (Number of values)
μ = (x₁ + x₂ + x₃ + ... + xₙ) / n
📝 Example: Student Test Scores

Five students scored: 75, 82, 90, 68, 85

Mean = (75 + 82 + 90 + 68 + 85) / 5 = 400 / 5 = 80

The average score is 80.

When to use Mean: Best for data without extreme outliers (very high or very low values that don't represent the typical data).

2. Median (Middle Value)

The median is the middle value when data is arranged in order. If there are two middle values, take their average.

📝 Example: House Prices in a Neighborhood

Seven houses sold for: $200,000, $220,000, $215,000, $950,000, $210,000, $225,000, $205,000

Step 1: Arrange in order: $200,000, $205,000, $210,000, $215,000, $220,000, $225,000, $950,000

Median = $215,000 (the middle value)

Mean = $317,857 (much higher due to the $950,000 house)

Notice how the median ($215,000) better represents typical house prices than the mean!

When to use Median: Best when you have outliers or skewed data. It's not affected by extreme values.

3. Mode (Most Frequent)

The mode is the value that appears most frequently in the dataset.

📝 Example: Shoe Sizes in a Store

Sizes sold today: 7, 8, 8, 9, 7, 8, 10, 8, 9, 8

Mode = 8 (appears 5 times, more than any other size)

This helps the store know which size to stock more of!

When to use Mode: Best for categorical data or when you want to know the most common occurrence.

Measures of Dispersion

Dispersion measures tell us how spread out our data is. Are all values close together or scattered widely?

1. Range

The range is simply the difference between the highest and lowest values.

Range = Maximum value - Minimum value
📝 Example: Daily Temperatures

Temperatures this week: 68°F, 72°F, 75°F, 70°F, 73°F, 69°F, 71°F

Range = 75°F - 68°F = 7°F

The temperature varied by 7 degrees this week.

2. Variance

Variance measures how far each value is from the mean, on average. It gives us a precise measure of spread.

Variance (σ²) = Σ(x - μ)² / n
Where: x = each value, μ = mean, n = number of values
📝 Example: Calculating Variance

Data: 4, 8, 6, 5, 3

Step 1: Find mean = (4+8+6+5+3)/5 = 5.2

Step 2: Find each deviation from mean:

  • (4 - 5.2)² = 1.44
  • (8 - 5.2)² = 7.84
  • (6 - 5.2)² = 0.64
  • (5 - 5.2)² = 0.04
  • (3 - 5.2)² = 4.84

Step 3: Variance = (1.44+7.84+0.64+0.04+4.84)/5 = 2.96

3. Standard Deviation (SD)

Standard deviation is the square root of variance. It's easier to interpret because it's in the same units as our original data.

Standard Deviation (σ) = √Variance
📝 Example: Continuing from Above

Variance = 2.96

Standard Deviation = √2.96 = 1.72

This means values typically differ from the mean by about 1.72 units.

Interpretation: A small SD means data is clustered close to the mean. A large SD means data is spread out widely.
Same mean, different spread: the standard deviation shows how tightly values cluster around the mean.

Interpreting Descriptive Statistics

Low Variability Dataset

Exam scores: 78, 79, 80, 81, 82

Mean = 80, SD = 1.41

Interpretation: Students performed consistently. Most scored close to 80.

High Variability Dataset

Exam scores: 50, 65, 80, 95, 100

Mean = 78, SD = 18.71

Interpretation: Students performed very differently. Wide range of abilities.

Calculating Measures Using Tools

Manual Calculation Tips

  1. Always organize your data first (sort it in order)
  2. Use a calculator or spreadsheet for large datasets
  3. Double-check your work, especially with variance calculations
  4. Round final answers appropriately (usually 2 decimal places)

Z-Scores and the Normal Distribution Added

A z-score tells how many standard deviations a value is from the mean, so values from different scales can be compared.

z = (x − mean) / standard deviation
📝 Example: Test Scores

Scores 75, 82, 90, 68, 85 have mean = 80 and sample SD = 8.63 (squared deviations sum to 298; 298 ÷ 4 = 74.5; √74.5 = 8.63).

A value with |z| > 3 is usually treated as an outlier. In Excel: =STANDARDIZE(90, 80, 8.63) gives 1.16.

The Normal Distribution and the 68-95-99.7 (empirical) rule.
📝 Example: Empirical Rule

Delivery times are normal with mean 30 min and SD 5 min.

  • 68% of deliveries take 25 to 35 minutes (30 ± 5).
  • 95% take 20 to 40 minutes (30 ± 10).
  • 99.7% take 15 to 45 minutes (30 ± 15). A 50-minute delivery has z = (50−30)/5 = +4, a clear outlier.

Coefficient of Variation (CV) Added

CV = (Standard deviation / Mean) × 100%

CV compares the spread of datasets with different units or scales. Test scores above: 8.63 / 80 = 10.8%. If another class has mean 60 and SD 12, its CV is 20%, so its scores are relatively more spread out even though the SD gap looks modest. Lower CV means more consistent data.

2. Correlation & Trend Analysis3 Hours

Understanding Correlation

Correlation measures the relationship between two variables. It tells us if two things tend to change together.

Types of Correlation

✅ Positive Correlation

When one variable increases, the other also increases.

Example: Study time and exam scores. More study time → Higher scores.

❌ Negative Correlation

When one variable increases, the other decreases.

Example: Speed and travel time. Faster speed → Less time.

⭕ No Correlation

Variables don't have a consistent relationship.

Example: Shoe size and IQ. No relationship expected.

Important: Correlation does NOT mean causation! Just because two things are correlated doesn't mean one causes the other.
📝 Example: Ice Cream Sales and Drowning

Ice cream sales and drowning incidents show a positive correlation.

Does ice cream cause drowning? NO!

Both increase in summer due to hot weather (the real cause). This is called a "spurious correlation."

Calculating Correlation Coefficients

The correlation coefficient (r) measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship. It ranges from -1 to +1.

r = -1 to +1
Where:
+1 = Perfect positive correlation
0 = No correlation
-1 = Perfect negative correlation

Interpreting Correlation Coefficient

📝 Example: Advertising Spend vs Sales

A company calculates r = 0.85 between advertising spend and sales.

Interpretation:

  • Very strong positive correlation
  • When advertising increases, sales tend to increase significantly
  • 85% of the relationship is linear

Introduction to Trend Analysis

Trend analysis helps us identify patterns in data over time. It answers: "Is this going up, down, or staying the same?"

Types of Trends

  1. Upward Trend: Values increase over time (e.g., company revenue growing)
  2. Downward Trend: Values decrease over time (e.g., product returns declining)
  3. Horizontal/Flat Trend: Values stay relatively constant
  4. Seasonal Trend: Regular patterns that repeat (e.g., holiday sales spikes)
  5. Cyclical Trend: Long-term up and down patterns (e.g., economic cycles)
📝 Example: Monthly Website Visitors

Identifying Patterns and Trends Over Time

Steps to Identify Trends

  1. Collect time-series data: Gather data points at regular intervals
  2. Plot the data: Create a line graph to visualize changes
  3. Look for patterns: Identify general direction and any repeating patterns
  4. Calculate moving averages: Smooth out short-term fluctuations
  5. Make predictions: Use the trend to forecast future values

Observations:

  • Overall trend: Upward (sales increasing year-over-year)
  • Seasonal pattern: Q4 always highest (holiday shopping)
  • Dip in Q3: Consistent pattern (back-to-school competition?)

Business insight: Prepare extra inventory for Q4, plan promotions for Q3 to boost sales.

Practical Tip: Always look at multiple time periods before confirming a trend. One or two data points don't make a trend!

3. Introduction to Hypothesis Testing3 Hours

What is Hypothesis Testing?

Hypothesis testing is a statistical method to make decisions about a population based on sample data. It's like being a detective – you have a theory, and you use evidence to support or reject it.

📝 Real-World Example

Scenario: A pharmaceutical company claims their new medicine reduces headaches within 30 minutes for 80% of patients.

Question: Is this claim true, or are they exaggerating?

Solution: We use hypothesis testing! Test the medicine on a sample of patients and see if the results support the claim.

Null and Alternative Hypotheses

Every hypothesis test starts with two competing statements:

Null Hypothesis (H₀)

The null hypothesis is the "status quo" or "default" position. It usually states that there is NO effect or NO difference.

H₀: There is no significant effect/difference
(The claim we're trying to disprove)

Alternative Hypothesis (H₁ or Hₐ)

The alternative hypothesis is what we're trying to prove. It states there IS an effect or difference.

H₁: There is a significant effect/difference
(What we believe is true)
📝 Example: New Teaching Method

Situation: A school wants to test if a new teaching method improves test scores.

Current average score: 75%

H₀ (Null Hypothesis): The new teaching method does NOT improve scores. Average score remains 75%.

H₁ (Alternative Hypothesis): The new teaching method DOES improve scores. Average score is greater than 75%.

We collect data, run the test, and either reject H₀ (the new method works!) or fail to reject H₀ (not enough evidence).

Think of it Like a Trial

H₀: Defendant is innocent (presumed)

H₁: Defendant is guilty

We need strong evidence to reject innocence!

Business Example

H₀: New ad campaign has no effect on sales

H₁: New ad campaign increases sales

Test with data before making big investments!

Type I and Type II Errors

Overlapping distributions: moving the critical value trades Type I risk against Type II risk.

In hypothesis testing, we can make two types of mistakes:

Type I Error (False Positive) - α

Rejecting the null hypothesis when it's actually true. We say there's an effect when there isn't.

📝 Example: Type I Error

Medical test scenario: Test says you have a disease, but you're actually healthy.

Business scenario: You conclude the new product is better, but it's actually the same. You waste money launching it.

Type II Error (False Negative) - β

Failing to reject the null hypothesis when it's actually false. We say there's NO effect when there IS one.

📝 Example: Type II Error

Medical test scenario: Test says you're healthy, but you actually have the disease.

Business scenario: You conclude the new marketing strategy doesn't work, but it actually does. You miss an opportunity.

Significance Level (α): Usually set at 0.05 (5%). This means we accept a 5% chance of making a Type I error. It's our tolerance for being wrong.

Basic Steps in Hypothesis Testing

Decision rule: if p-value ≤ α reject H0; otherwise fail to reject H0.
The 5-Step Process:
  1. State the Hypotheses (H₀ and H₁)

    Clearly define what you're testing.

  2. Choose Significance Level (α)

    Usually 0.05 (5% risk of Type I error).

  3. Collect Data and Calculate Test Statistic

    Gather sample data and calculate the appropriate test statistic (e.g., t-statistic, z-statistic).

  4. Determine the p-value

    The p-value tells us the probability of getting our results if H₀ is true.

  5. Make a Decision

    If p-value ≤ α: Reject H₀ (evidence supports H₁)

    If p-value > α: Fail to reject H₀ (not enough evidence)

📝 Complete Example: Coffee Shop Wait Time

Problem: A coffee shop claims average wait time is 5 minutes. Customers complain it's longer. Test this claim.

Step 1: State Hypotheses

  • H₀: Average wait time = 5 minutes
  • H₁: Average wait time > 5 minutes

Step 2: Set Significance Level

  • α = 0.05 (5%)

Step 3: Collect Data

  • Sample 30 customers: Average wait = 5.8 minutes, SD = 1.2 minutes

Step 4: Calculate p-value

  • Using statistical software: t = 3.65 with 29 degrees of freedom, p-value = 0.0005 (one-tailed). See the manual calculation in the "Worked t-test" example below

Step 5: Make Decision

  • p-value (0.002) < α (0.05)
  • Decision: Reject H₀
  • Conclusion: Evidence shows wait time is significantly longer than 5 minutes. The shop needs to improve service!
Remember: "Failing to reject H₀" is NOT the same as "accepting H₀." It just means we don't have enough evidence to say H₀ is wrong. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence!

Standard Error and Confidence Intervals Added

The standard error (SE) measures how much a sample mean varies from sample to sample. A confidence interval (CI) is a range likely to contain the true population mean.

SE = s / √n
CI = x̄ ± t* × SE
📝 Example: Coffee Shop Wait Time (n = 30, mean = 5.8, SD = 1.2)
  • SE = 1.2 / √30 = 1.2 / 5.477 = 0.219
  • t* for 95% confidence, 29 degrees of freedom = 2.045
  • Margin of error = 2.045 × 0.219 = 0.448
  • 95% CI = 5.8 ± 0.448 = (5.35, 6.25) minutes

The interval does not contain 5, which agrees with rejecting H₀: mean = 5. Excel: =CONFIDENCE.T(0.05, 1.2, 30) returns 0.448.

Reading a CI: "We are 95% confident the true mean lies in this range" means that 95% of intervals built this way would capture the true mean. A larger sample or lower variability gives a narrower interval.
Confidence interval for the coffee shop wait time (minutes).

Worked t-test (Manual Calculation) Added

t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s / √n) = (5.8 − 5) / (1.2 / √30) = 0.8 / 0.219 = 3.65

Degrees of freedom = n − 1 = 29. The one-tailed critical value at α = 0.05 is 1.699. Since 3.65 > 1.699 (equivalently p = 0.0005 < 0.05), reject H₀. Excel: =T.DIST.RT(3.65, 29) gives 0.0005.

One-tailed test: the observed t-statistic falls deep inside the rejection region, so the p-value is tiny.

Worked Chi-Square Test of Independence Added

📝 Does product preference depend on gender?

H₀: preference and gender are independent. H₁: they are related. α = 0.05.

ObservedProduct AProduct BTotal
Male302050
Female203050
Total5050100

Expected count per cell = (row total × column total) / grand total = 50 × 50 / 100 = 25.

χ² = Σ (Observed − Expected)² / Expected = 4 × (5² / 25) = 4.0

Degrees of freedom = (rows−1)(columns−1) = 1; critical value at α = 0.05 is 3.84. Since 4.0 > 3.84 (p = 0.0455), reject H₀: preference is associated with gender. Excel: =CHISQ.DIST.RT(4, 1).

ANOVA: Comparing Three or More Means Added

Running many t-tests inflates the Type I error. ANOVA tests all groups at once using the F-ratio: variation between groups divided by variation within groups.

📝 Delivery Time (minutes) from Three Warehouses

A: 5, 6, 7 (mean 6) · B: 8, 9, 10 (mean 9) · C: 4, 5, 6 (mean 5) · Grand mean = 6.67

SourceSum of squaresdfMean squareFp-value
Between groups2621313.00.0066
Within groups661

Between SS = 3 × [(6−6.67)² + (9−6.67)² + (5−6.67)²] = 26. Within SS = 2 + 2 + 2 = 6. Since p = 0.0066 < 0.05, at least one warehouse mean differs. A follow-up (post-hoc) test such as Tukey's shows which one: here B is slowest.

Statistical vs practical significance: with a very large sample even a tiny difference gives p < 0.05. Always report the effect size (difference in means, r, CI) and ask whether it matters for the business.

Choosing Appropriate Visualizations

Start with the question you want to answer, then pick the chart.

The right chart makes data easy to understand. The wrong chart confuses people. Here's how to choose:

Your Goal Best Chart Type When to Use
Show distribution Histogram, Box Plot Understanding how data is spread
Compare categories Bar Chart Comparing different groups
Show relationship Scatter Plot Finding correlation between variables
Show trend over time Line Chart Tracking changes across time
Show proportions Pie Chart Parts of a whole (use sparingly!)

Histograms for Distribution Analysis

A histogram shows how frequently different values appear in your data. It groups data into "bins" or ranges.

Purpose: Understand the shape and distribution of your data. Is it normal? Skewed? Are there multiple peaks?
📝 Example: Student Test Scores Histogram

100 students took a test. Scores grouped:

Score Range Number of Students
0-20 2
21-40 5
41-60 15
61-80 48
81-100 30

Interpretation: Most students scored between 61-80. The distribution is slightly left-skewed (more high scorers). Only a few students failed.

What to Look for in Histograms

  • Normal Distribution: Bell-shaped curve, symmetric
  • Right-Skewed: Long tail on the right (few very high values)
  • Left-Skewed: Long tail on the left (few very low values)
  • Bimodal: Two peaks (might indicate two different groups)
  • Uniform: Relatively flat (all values equally likely)

Box Plots for Data Spread and Outliers

Box plots (also called box-and-whisker plots) show five key numbers: minimum, Q1, median, Q3, and maximum. They're excellent for spotting outliers.

Five Number Summary:
Minimum | Q1 (25th percentile) | Median (50th percentile) | Q3 (75th percentile) | Maximum
📝 Example: Employee Salaries

Salary data for 50 employees (in thousands):

  • Minimum: $35k
  • Q1: $45k (25% earn less than this)
  • Median: $55k (middle value)
  • Q3: $68k (75% earn less than this)
  • Maximum: $120k
  • Outlier: One person at $120k (CEO perhaps?)

Interpretation:

  • Middle 50% of employees earn between $45k-$68k
  • The $120k salary is an outlier (much higher than others)
  • The box shows where most salaries cluster
Identifying Outliers: Values beyond 1.5 × IQR (Interquartile Range) from Q1 or Q3 are typically considered outliers.
IQR = Q3 - Q1

When to Use Box Plots

  • Comparing distributions across multiple groups
  • Identifying outliers quickly
  • Understanding data spread at a glance
  • When you have lots of data points

Scatter Plots for Relationship Analysis

Scatter plots show the relationship between two numerical variables. Each point represents one observation with values on both axes.

📝 Example: Study Hours vs Exam Score

Patterns in Scatter Plots

  • Positive Linear: Points form an upward-sloping line
  • Negative Linear: Points form a downward-sloping line
  • No Pattern: Points scattered randomly (no correlation)
  • Curved Pattern: Non-linear relationship
  • Clusters: Distinct groups visible
Pro Tip: Add a "trend line" (line of best fit) to scatter plots to make the relationship clearer.

Best Practices for Data Visualization

  1. Keep it Simple: Don't overcrowd with information
  2. Label Everything: Clear titles, axis labels, and legends
  3. Choose Colors Wisely: Use contrasting colors, avoid red-green for colorblind accessibility
  4. Start Y-axis at Zero: For bar charts (avoids misleading viewers)
  5. Show the Data: Include sample size and key statistics
  6. Context Matters: Add reference lines or benchmarks when helpful
Common Mistake: Using 3D charts when 2D would be clearer. 3D often distorts perception and makes data harder to read. Keep it flat!

Running Basic Hypothesis Tests in Excel (t-tests)

T-tests help determine if the difference between two groups is statistically significant.

Types of T-tests

  • One-sample t-test: Compare sample mean to a known value
  • Two-sample t-test: Compare means of two independent groups
  • Paired t-test: Compare means of the same group at different times
📝 Example: Testing New Training Program

Scenario: Did employees' productivity improve after training?

Data:

  • Column A: Productivity BEFORE training (A2:A21)
  • Column B: Productivity AFTER training (B2:B21)

Steps:

  1. Data → Data Analysis → t-Test: Paired Two Sample for Means
  2. Variable 1 Range: A2:A21 (before)
  3. Variable 2 Range: B2:B21 (after)
  4. Hypothesized Mean Difference: 0
  5. Alpha: 0.05
  6. Choose Output location → OK

Reading Results:

  • Look at "P(T<=t) two-tail" value
  • If p-value < 0.05: Training significantly improved productivity!
  • If p-value > 0.05: No significant improvement shown
Quick Decision Rule:
p-value < 0.05 = Statistically significant (reject null hypothesis)
p-value ≥ 0.05 = Not statistically significant (fail to reject null hypothesis)

7. Basics of Predictive Analysis Concepts1 Hour

Introduction to Predictive Modeling

Predictive modeling uses historical data to make predictions about future events. Instead of just describing what happened (descriptive analytics), we forecast what will happen.

Descriptive Analytics

"What happened?"

Last quarter sales were $2M

Average customer age is 35

Most popular product is Item X

Predictive Analytics

"What will happen?"

Next quarter sales will be $2.3M

Customer likely to churn: 65%

Item Y will be popular next month

📝 Real-World Example: Netflix Recommendations

Netflix uses predictive modeling to recommend shows:

  • Input data: Your viewing history, ratings, time spent watching, genres preferred
  • Model: Analyzes patterns from millions of users
  • Prediction: "You'll probably enjoy Show X" (with 85% confidence)
  • Result: More engaging experience, less time searching

Overview of Common Predictive Techniques

1. Regression Analysis

Predicts a continuous numerical value based on input variables.

Linear Regression:
Y = a + bX
Y = predicted value, X = input variable, a = intercept, b = slope
📝 Example: House Price Prediction

Goal: Predict house price based on square footage

Model: Price = $50,000 + ($150 × Square Feet)

Prediction for 2,000 sq ft house:

Price = $50,000 + ($150 × 2,000) = $350,000

Use cases:

  • Sales forecasting
  • Revenue prediction
  • Demand estimation
  • Risk assessment

2. Classification

Predicts which category something belongs to (Yes/No, A/B/C, etc.).

📝 Example: Email Spam Filter

Goal: Classify emails as "Spam" or "Not Spam"

Input features:

  • Contains words like "free," "winner," "click here"
  • Sender's email domain
  • Number of links
  • ALL CAPS usage

Model learns: If email has 3+ suspicious features → Classify as Spam

Business use cases:

  • Customer churn prediction (Will they leave? Yes/No)
  • Loan approval (Approve/Reject)
  • Product recommendations (Will they buy? Yes/No)
  • Disease diagnosis (Has disease? Yes/No)

3. Time Series Forecasting

Predicts future values based on historical time-based patterns.

📝 Example: Inventory Forecasting

Historical monthly sales:

Jan: 1,000 | Feb: 1,100 | Mar: 1,200 | Apr: 1,150 | May: 1,300 | Jun: 1,400

Pattern identified: Average growth of 8% per month

July prediction: 1,400 × 1.08 = 1,512 units

Action: Order 1,600 units for July (includes buffer) to avoid stockouts.

4. Clustering

Groups similar items together based on characteristics.

📝 Example: Customer Segmentation

A retail store analyzes customers and finds 3 distinct groups:

Cluster Characteristics Marketing Strategy
Budget Shoppers Low spend, high discount usage Send coupon offers
Premium Buyers High spend, brand loyal VIP program, exclusive previews
Occasional Buyers Medium spend, seasonal Targeted seasonal campaigns

Benefit: Personalized marketing improves conversion rates by 30%+

Understanding the Goal of Predictive Analysis

Primary Goals of Predictive Analytics:
  1. Forecast future outcomes: What will happen?
  2. Identify patterns: What trends exist in our data?
  3. Optimize decisions: What's the best action to take?
  4. Reduce risk: What problems can we prevent?
  5. Increase efficiency: How can we improve processes?
📝 Business Application: Retail Chain

Problem: Too much inventory in some stores, not enough in others

Predictive Solution:

  • Data collected: Sales history, weather, local events, holidays, store location
  • Model built: Predicts demand for each store daily
  • Action: Automatically redistribute inventory based on predictions
  • Result:
    • 20% reduction in excess inventory
    • 15% fewer stockouts
    • $2M annual savings

Steps to Build a Predictive Model (Simplified)

The predictive modeling lifecycle. In practice you loop back from evaluation to earlier steps.
  1. Define the problem

    What do you want to predict? Be specific!

  2. Collect data

    Gather historical data relevant to your prediction

  3. Clean and prepare data

    Remove errors, handle missing values, format consistently

  4. Choose a model

    Select appropriate technique (regression, classification, etc.)

  5. Train the model

    Feed historical data to the model so it learns patterns

  6. Test the model

    Evaluate accuracy on new data it hasn't seen before

  7. Deploy and monitor

    Use in real business scenarios and track performance

Evaluating Classification Models Added

📝 Customer Churn Predictions (100 customers)
Predicted: ChurnPredicted: Stay
Actual: Churn40 (true positive)5 (false negative)
Actual: Stay10 (false positive)45 (true negative)
  • Accuracy = (40 + 45) / 100 = 85%
  • Precision = 40 / (40 + 10) = 80% (of predicted churners, how many really churn)
  • Recall = 40 / (40 + 5) = 88.9% (of real churners, how many we caught)
  • F1 score = 2 × 0.80 × 0.889 / (0.80 + 0.889) = 0.84

When missing a churner is costly, favor recall; when a false alarm is costly, favor precision.
